Land Titles & Registration

Verbal instructions vs. written guidelines (G.O.)
A Government Order is published, numbered and citable; a verbal instruction exists only in the memory of whichever official handles your file. When a department is fixing a land-record error through oral instructions rather than a written order — as revenue officials reportedly are on Section 22A — two identical properties can get two different outcomes depending purely on who answers the phone. — taught by: the 22A crisis’s verbal-corrections report
The three-bottleneck problem in land acquisition
Land acquisition (paying for and transferring title), R&R (resettling displaced people) and forest clearance (where an alignment crosses forest land) are three separate approval chains that must all clear before a project proceeds — a government “war footing” order is only as fast as its weakest chain, usually R&R or forest clearance, not the acquisition paperwork itself. — taught by: the Irrigation Minister’s land-acquisition war-footing order
Registration — why it’s everything
You legally own immovable property only once the sale deed is registered with stamp duty paid — not merely once you’ve paid for it. Block registration and you’ve effectively frozen the property. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Prohibited list (Section 22A)
A district-wise register of survey numbers sub-registrars must refuse to register — legitimately covering government, endowment/wakf and ceiling-surplus land, but dangerous because it works on survey numbers, which can wrongly sweep up private land sharing or mistyped into a listed number. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Colony-level over-listing (22A)
The specific harm Harish Rao attacked: instead of one stray survey number, entire registered residential colonies get added to the 22A list in a batch — freezing hundreds of clean, patta homes at once so owners can’t sell or raise a loan. — taught by: Harish Rao’s 22A Assembly threat
Relaxation / release rate
The government’s own scorecard for a prohibited-list crisis: of the total relaxation (release) applications filed, how many are actually cleared versus rejected. Telangana’s Revenue Minister quoting “98% cleared, only 12–13 of 300+ rejected” is this exact metric — a number worth checking again once independent audits exist, since a self-reported clearance rate is not the same as a verified one. — taught by: Ponguleti’s 22A rebuttal
De-notification
The formal process of striking a property off a prohibited/restricted list so it becomes transactable again — the exact step the alleged “30% commission” racket sits on top of. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Patta land
Land for which the owner holds a formal government-issued title deed, as opposed to disputed, assigned, or government land — the category Section 22A is meant to exclude, and the category getting wrongly trapped by it. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Regularisation, GOs and memos
Governments legalise occupied or exempted land over the decades through Government Orders — but a regularised parcel is only clean on paper if that GO is attached to its record. Collect the GO/memo history, not just the sale deed. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
TSLR vs GHMC numbering
Hyderabad has two parallel address systems — a 1969–70 Town Survey Land Records numbering and GHMC’s own ward/block numbers. Mismatches between the two have turned private homes into “government land” on paper. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
ULC (Urban Land Ceiling)
A repealed law that once capped urban landholding; the exemption/surrender records it left behind still matter, since several exempted parcels were wrongly re-listed for want of an entered exemption. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Waqf land
Property permanently donated for Muslim religious purposes under a central Act — state revenue officers cannot simply delete waqf entries, so colonies near old disputed waqf land face the slowest 22A-style resolution. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Dharani vs Bhu Bharati
Dharani was the previous government’s land-records portal; Bhu Bharati is the current replacement. Errors made migrating data between the two are the government’s own explanation for the 22A crisis. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Khata (and a khata freeze)
The municipal record that puts a property on the local tax/ownership rolls — without it you can’t cleanly get a bank loan, utility connections, or resell. Freezing khata issuance on a batch of sites (as happened with 1,100 MUDA plots) makes them unsellable overnight even though the sale deed still exists. — taught by: the MUDA cancellation’s implementation
Cancellation vs title
An allotment-based title can be undone at the root: if the original government allotment was illegal, everything built on it since — deeds, registrations, loans — can collapse. Registration records a transaction; it doesn’t purify a rotten source. — taught by: the MUDA scam
Development authority (built-in conflict)
Bodies like MUDA, BDA and HMDA both make the rules (approvals) and play the game (own and sell land) — a structural conflict of interest that land scandals tend to grow from. — taught by: the MUDA scam
Alternative-site / 50:50 compensation
Paying a landowner in developed plots instead of cash when their raw land is acquired — the honest version is transparent and formula-based (like land pooling); the abused version becomes a discretionary currency for favours. — taught by: the MUDA scam
Land Pooling (60:40 model)
Telangana’s proposed statewide alternative to forced acquisition: instead of paying cash for raw land, the government “pools” it, develops the layout (roads, drainage, plots), and gives the original owner back 60% of the fully-developed plots — keeping 40% to sell and fund the infrastructure. Cleaner than 50:50 discretionary compensation because the ratio is fixed in advance, not negotiated case by case — but only as clean as the bill’s actual text turns out to be. — taught by: the Assembly’s 7 September land-pooling agenda
Commission of Inquiry
A judge-led fact-finding body that can recommend but not punish — its power is legitimacy: acting on a judge’s report is politically defensible, ignoring one is costly. — taught by: the MUDA scam
JDA and benami (the coercive version)
A Joint Development Agreement — landowner contributes land, developer builds, they split the result — is your core, legal business. The dirty cousin: get land blacklisted, owner turns desperate, a “partner” appears offering 50:50 through a benami (name-lender) entity. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Restoration vs redevelopment
Restoration repairs what exists; redevelopment replaces what failed. Good heritage projects mix both instead of bulldozing everything. — taught by: the Mir Alam Mandi revamp
Phased execution
Build a section, shift occupants in, build the next — slower and costlier per square foot, but livelihoods survive the construction, unlike a clear-everyone-out-and-build-fast approach. — taught by: the Mir Alam Mandi revamp
Reserve price vs sale price
A published reserve is only the floor an auctioneer will accept — the actual winning bid in a competitive e-auction can land well above it, as TGIIC’s Raidurg auctions have now shown three times running. — taught by: ITI’s Bengaluru land e-auction; confirmed again by the Raidurg record
PSU land monetisation / NLMC
The National Land Monetization Corporation is the central vehicle that professionally values and auctions off surplus land held by old public-sector companies inside cities that grew up around their factories/offices — a leading government-supply signal for local land markets. — taught by: ITI’s Bengaluru land e-auction
RMP zoning
Bengaluru’s statutory Revised Master Plan tags every parcel with a permitted use; a “Commercial (Business)” tag means a buyer can build offices without a fresh land-use conversion — a major value driver baked into the price. — taught by: ITI’s Bengaluru land e-auction
TGIIC vs HMDA
Two different Telangana government bodies both sell/auction Hyderabad-area land — TGIIC out of an industrial-development mandate, HMDA out of an urban-planning one. Knowing which one you’re dealing with matters, since their land banks, rules and pricing patterns differ. — taught by: the Raidurg auction record
MSTC e-auction platform
A central PSU-run online bidding portal used by many government bodies to run transparent land/asset auctions — competitively bid, published-reserve pricing that’s far harder to under-report than a privately negotiated sale, which is why auction results become the market’s reference price. — taught by: the Raidurg auction record
RERA Section 10 (agent liability)
RERA doesn’t just regulate builders — a registered real-estate agent who markets or books units in a project that isn’t itself RERA-registered can personally be ordered to refund buyers with interest. “I was just the broker” is not a defence. — taught by: a TG-RERA refund order
Alienation of protected land
In Indian land law, “alienation” means the legal transfer of ownership or rights out of a protected/public category — via sale, lease, gift, exchange or mortgage — into a different use. It doesn’t mean abandonment; it means converting the land’s legal status, which is exactly what Karnataka’s new park-land bill would let government do to up to 5% of any park, once it actually clears the Assembly. — taught by: Karnataka’s park-land alienation bill
FTL & buffer zone
Full Tank Level is the highest water level a lake, tank or river stretch is engineered to hold; the buffer zone is a protective ring of undeveloped land around it that keeps the water body’s catchment and flood path clear. Land inside either category is legally fragile — even a clean-looking patta can’t override an FTL/buffer classification once a parcel is formally surveyed into it. — taught by: the Musi land-pooling directive
TDR (Transferable Development Rights)
Instead of paying cash when the government takes your land for a public project, it can issue a TDR certificate — the right to build extra floor area on a different plot you own, or to sell that right to someone else. It only has real value if there’s a plot to use it on and a working market to sell it in. Telangana has since used the same TDR mechanism the other way round too — letting tall-building developers meet part of their floor-area obligation with TDR instead of cash, at a relaxed 3% (10–20 floors) or 5% (above 20 floors) of built-up area. — taught by: the Musi land-pooling directive; Telangana’s high-rise TDR relaxation
Shell company / benami holding
A shell company exists mostly on paper with little real operation, sometimes used to disguise who actually owns an asset like land. When done to hide the true owner, it is called a benami arrangement and is illegal under India’s Benami Transactions Act. — taught by: Konda Surekha cabinet fallout
Conditional registration
Allowing a sale to be registered while the deed itself states that ownership remains subject to the outcome of a pending court case — a middle path between an outright registration block and a clean, unqualified title. — taught by: Vivek’s Deep Dive of the Day (Paigah land)
Civil Suit (CS) number
The case-tracking number Indian courts assign a lawsuit, used ever after as shorthand for that dispute and everything it covers — “CS-14 of 1958” still governs a specific Hyderabad estate’s ownership today, 68 years on. — taught by: Vivek’s Deep Dive of the Day (Paigah land)
Government-to-government land swap
Instead of buying land on the open market, one government body trades surplus or unused land directly with another for a project it needs — here, TGSRTC depot land at RTC Crossroads for housing, in exchange for acreage at Gajularamaram. — taught by: the RTC Crossroads – Indiramma Towers land swap

Building & Apartment Law

RERA Section 8 (project takeover by allottees)
When a registered project’s promoter defaults, the regulator can hand day-to-day completion to the project’s own buyers’ association instead of leaving them with only a compensation claim against a promoter who may have no money left — and a regulator can later carve out narrower relief (like registration for specific units) on a separate, faster timeline from the main completion order. — taught by: the TG-RERA Jaya Diamonds order
Encroachment vs. ownership dispute
An enforcement agency’s demolition power is legally premised on land being encroached — on a lake buffer, road alignment or similarly protected category — not on ordinary private land. Whether a specific plot actually falls in such a category, or whether the boundary was misjudged, is exactly what a court case turns on, and exactly what a single complaint video cannot settle either way. — taught by: the Gandi Maisamma HYDRAA complaint
RWA (Residents’ Welfare Association) mandate
The single legally-recognised body representing all owners in a completed apartment project. Karnataka’s new law requires exactly one per project, even multi-phase ones, closing a loophole where developers split large complexes into multiple weak associations to dodge accountability. — taught by: Karnataka’s Apartment Ownership Bill
75% redevelopment consent
The ownership-majority threshold a Karnataka apartment complex now needs to approve redevelopment. Owners who refuse can still be bought out, but only at twice independently-assessed market value — protecting holdouts without letting one owner block an entire rebuild. — taught by: Karnataka’s Apartment Ownership Bill
Structural safety audit
A certified engineer’s mandatory, repeating (every 5 years) inspection for any building over 30 years old — a paper trail a buyer should now always ask for on an older Bengaluru apartment. — taught by: Karnataka’s Apartment Ownership Bill
Private-road municipal takeover
A municipality can now declare a poorly-maintained layout’s internal road a “public road” and take over its upkeep — with zero compensation to whoever owned it — the moment it’s judged a public-safety risk. — taught by: Karnataka’s municipal amendment bills
Plot-size vs sanctioned-height rule
Hyderabad’s enforcement trigger for small plots: below 75 sq. yards, anything built over 7 metres (roughly ground-plus-one) needs full building permission regardless of what was originally sanctioned; above 75 sq. yards, any construction with zero permission at all is the violation. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
Encroachment on a nala (storm-water drain)
Building even partly over a natural drainage channel is a double violation — it steals land that was never the owner’s to build on, and it removes water’s only path during heavy rain, which is why nala-encroaching structures are treated as both illegal and structurally suspect. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
HYDRAA
Hyderabad Disaster Response and Assets Monitoring and Protection Agency — a Telangana government body created to identify and demolish unsafe, illegal or encroaching structures across the city, working alongside (not instead of) the local municipal corporation’s own Town Planning wing. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
UC (unauthorised construction) notice
A formal notice a municipal Town Planning wing serves on a building it finds was constructed without permission, or beyond what was permitted — the first legal step before seizure or demolition. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
Citizen-tip enforcement (MyCURE / Prajavani)
Telangana’s municipal complaint apps let any resident report suspected illegal construction with photos and a location pin; officials now actively cross-check these tips against field inspections to decide where enforcement teams go next. — taught by: Cyberabad’s widening demolition drive
Pile and pile cap
Deep concrete columns (piles) driven into soft or waterlogged ground to reach solid soil, topped by a concrete slab (pile cap) that spreads the load evenly — the standard foundation method near lakes and rivers. — taught by: Mir Alam Tank inspection story
Pier-cum-pier cap
The vertical support column of a bridge (pier) plus the horizontal beam on top of it (pier cap) that the road deck rests on — progress on these tells you how much of a bridge’s structural skeleton is ready, exactly the kind of structure the Moosarambagh high-level bridge over the Musi is built from. — taught by: Mir Alam Tank inspection story; the Moosarambagh bridge inauguration
C&D waste (Construction & Demolition waste)
Debris like broken concrete, bricks, sand and tiles left over from building or demolishing structures, often dumped illegally on vacant plots. — taught by: GBA vacant plot clean-up drive
Cost-recovery-via-property-tax mechanism
A civic enforcement tool where the authority does required clean-up or compliance work itself and adds the cost directly to the owner’s property tax bill, rather than relying only on fines. — taught by: GBA vacant plot clean-up drive
Property expo as a market sentiment gauge
A developer-run property show’s theme, scale and stall focus is a real-time, informal signal of what builders themselves believe about demand — a defensive or reassuring slogan often means the trade body is trying to counter a slowdown narrative rather than reporting neutral fact, exactly the note CREDAI Hyderabad’s “rates won’t stop, demand won’t drop” theme struck this week. — taught by: CREDAI Property Show story
RERA delay interest (Rule 15)
When a registered project misses its promised possession date, the buyer is entitled to interest on money already paid, at a formula rate — typically the State Bank of India’s MCLR plus 2% — running from each payment date until possession. A statutory entitlement, not a discretionary favour. — taught by: the TG-RERA Vasavi Group interest order
RERA Section 3 (registration before any sale)
The core rule a project must clear before a promoter can advertise, market, book or sell a single unit: register it with the state RERA authority first. Selling under a “pre-launch” or “soft-launch” offer before that registration exists isn’t just risky — it is illegal by definition. — taught by: Vivek’s Deep Dive of the Day (Bharathi Builders, Kompally)
Defaulter Promoter (RERA)
A public listing a state RERA authority can put a builder’s name on after serious violations — pre-launch sales without registration, fund diversion, fraudulent advertising, failure to refund. Once listed, the promoter is barred from registering fresh projects, restricted from fresh allotments, and the listing itself becomes a free background check for any buyer, lender or broker who checks it before paying. — taught by: Vivek’s Deep Dive of the Day (Bharathi Builders, Kompally)
Pre-launch scheme vs registered sale
A pre-launch offer collects money on the promise of a future project, before land approvals, RERA registration or construction exist — the discount buyers are chasing is really an unpriced bet that everything downstream goes right. — taught by: Vivek’s Deep Dive of the Day (Bharathi Builders, Kompally)

Government Money & Institutions

Royalty share (mining auctions)
The percentage of a mineral’s value a winning bidder pays the government as royalty — the specific commercial term that determines how profitable a block actually needs to be to justify the exploration spend behind it. SCCL committed a 37.7% royalty share for its new Karnataka gold-and-copper block. — taught by: SCCL’s Devadurga gold-copper block
Centrally-sponsored scheme funding
Many large Indian welfare schemes (housing, rural employment, health) are jointly funded, with states implementing and the Centre releasing its share on its own schedule — that schedule becoming a live political flashpoint is common whenever the state and central governments are run by different parties, as with a year of zero central disbursement on Telangana’s Indiramma Housing scheme. — taught by: the Indiramma Housing funds row
Welfare-corporation scam
State governments run development corporations (for SCs, STs, minorities, backward classes) that hold budgeted welfare money in bank accounts. The classic fraud: funds are quietly transferred out of the corporation’s account into a web of shell/personal accounts, then allegedly recycled — here, ₹89 crore transferred out, an overall ~₹187 crore diversion alleged from the Valmiki (ST) Corporation. — taught by: the Valmiki Corporation scam
Revenue deficit
Daily income minus daily bills, with no asset creation either side — a state running one is borrowing just to pay salaries and pensions, like taking a loan for groceries. Telangana’s own mid-year CAG snapshot put this at ₹19,266.83 crore just four months into the financial year — a live constraint on how much cash is left over for land acquisition and infrastructure spend. — taught by: Telangana’s CAG report
Fiscal deficit
The full year’s borrowing need, including money spent building assets. Borrowing to build a road can pay for itself; borrowing for groceries can’t — the ratio between the two deficits tells you which kind of borrower a state is. — taught by: Telangana’s CAG report
Committed expenditure
Salaries, pensions and interest — the untouchable first claim on every rupee. When it eats the budget, infrastructure that moves land prices fights for scraps, and so, sometimes, does compensation for land the government has already taken. — taught by: Telangana’s CAG report; the Telangana High Court’s land-loser compensation query
Supplementary budget
Extra spending a government asks the legislature to approve mid-year, on top of the original annual budget — usually for unplanned or newly prioritised items. Karnataka’s ₹7,042.69-crore supplementary estimate, with ₹1,000 crore specifically for Bengaluru roads, is exactly this. — taught by: Karnataka’s supplementary budget
Devolution / Finance Commission share
Every five years a Finance Commission decides what slice of central taxes each state gets. A state that grows faster and controls population better can still see its share shrink, since formulas partly weight population — Karnataka says this cost it ₹65,000 crore over six years. — taught by: the Southern Zonal Council
Schedule IX / Schedule X institutions
Lists of government companies and institutions the 2014 AP Reorganisation Act said must be divided between Telangana and Andhra Pradesh — 23 of them remain undivided twelve years on. — taught by: the Southern Zonal Council
Mines and Minerals (Development and Regulation) Act
The central law governing who can tax and regulate mineral extraction. States want more taxing power over mining within their own territory; the Centre has historically resisted for the sake of national uniformity. — taught by: the Southern Zonal Council
Political clearance (MEA)
Before any Chief Minister travels abroad, the Ministry of External Affairs must clear both the trip and its itinerary. The Centre can approve one leg of a multi-country tour and deny another of the very same trip, and the state has no legal recourse to override it. — taught by: the Revanth US clearance denial
G.O. (Government Order) as institutional restructuring
A single G.O. can fold entire departments or classes of institutions into a new body overnight, without a legislative vote — the same sweeping-order power that created the Section 22A mess reshaped technical education via GO 97. — taught by: the GO 97 protests
Article 371J / regional development boards
A special constitutional provision giving Karnataka’s historically underdeveloped Kalyana Karnataka region its own dedicated development fund and a minister-in-charge, separate from general state administration. — taught by: Minister Nagendra’s additional charge
Academic diplomacy / MoU tourism
A state government courting foreign universities and investors through headline-friendly Memoranda of Understanding — genuinely useful for long-term ties, but an MoU is a statement of intent, not a binding, funded commitment. — taught by: Revanth’s Cambridge/Oxford visits
Mid-year fiscal snapshot (CAG)
The CAG publishes running totals of a state’s actual income and spending well before the financial year ends — an early-warning read that helps explain why land auctions, land pooling and TDR keep getting pushed as the government’s preferred revenue tools. — taught by: the CAG four-month report
Pay Revision Commission (PRC)
A periodic government-appointed panel that reviews and recommends how much state employees should be paid, including allowances and retirement benefits — implementing its report can cost a state thousands of crores a year. — taught by: statewide employee protests, Harish Rao detained
Old Pension Scheme (OPS) vs Contributory Pension Scheme (CPS)

NRI, Tax & Valuation

FEMA and the NRI farmland ban
The Foreign Exchange Management Act, 1999 lets NRIs and OCIs buy Indian flats, houses, offices and shops freely, with no RBI approval and no cap — but bans them from buying agricultural land, farmhouses or plantation property outright, through any route, sale deed or otherwise. Break it and the transaction can be voided, with Enforcement Directorate action and penalties up to three times the value. — taught by: today’s Land School (NRI guide to buying land)
NRE vs NRO vs FCNR accounts
The three bank accounts an NRI buyer routes property money through: NRE holds foreign earnings and is fully repatriable; NRO holds India-earned money and is capped on the way out; FCNR holds foreign-currency deposits. Which one funds the purchase today decides how freely the sale proceeds can leave India years later. — taught by: today’s Land School (NRI guide to buying land)
Repatriation cap (USD 1 million a year)
Money in an NRO account can be sent abroad only up to USD 1 million per financial year, subject to tax compliance and Forms 15CA/15CB — a hard ceiling that makes the funding-account choice at purchase time a real financial decision, not paperwork. — taught by: today’s Land School (NRI guide to buying land)
Guideline value (a.k.a. circle rate / DLC rate)
A state government’s own published minimum benchmark price per unit of land, revised periodically — it sets the floor for stamp duty and registration, regardless of what the buyer and seller actually agreed to pay. Market value is what a willing buyer will actually pay; the two rarely match exactly. — taught by: today’s Land School (guideline value vs market value)

If a property’s declared sale price is more than a set tolerance below its guideline value, the taxman treats the shortfall as the buyer’s taxable income under this Income Tax Act section — a trap that catches genuine below-market deals just as easily as underhand ones, since the law can’t tell the two apart from the numbers alone. — taught by: today’s Land School (guideline value vs market value)

1 · The story, explained from zero — sentence by sentence

S1 · Friday’s meeting was the second attempt, not the first
The KRMB’s three-member committee — chaired by Member Secretary Satish Kamboj, with Telangana Engineer-in-Chief O.V. Ramesh Babu and Andhra Pradesh Engineer-in-Chief K. Narasimha Murthy as the other two members — met in Hyderabad on Friday 4 September 2026, two weeks after an earlier sitting on 24 August had already ended without agreement.

Plain English — The same three officials sat down for a second try at dividing this year’s water, having already failed once.

Mechanics — This is a technical, engineer-level committee, not the full KRMB Board — it exists specifically to work out operational water-sharing numbers for Srisailam and Nagarjuna Sagar season by season, leaving bigger jurisdictional and legal questions to the Board and, ultimately, to the two state governments and the Centre.

Incentives — Each Engineer-in-Chief comes to the table representing his state government’s official position, not a neutral technical view — there is no incentive for either to move off his opening number before the state's political leadership signals it should.

Numbers — Two meetings in two weeks (24 August, 4 September) with no change in outcome; corroborated by Deccan Chronicle and Telangana Today.
S2 · Telangana’s ask: 50:50 and 200 TMC
Telangana’s Engineer-in-Chief demanded an equal 50:50 split of available Krishna water and asked specifically for 200 TMC (thousand million cubic feet) of water for Telangana this year, citing El Niño-linked drought risk as the reason the state needs a guaranteed volume rather than a vague promise.

Plain English — Telangana wants half the water, and wants a firm number, not just a principle, written down.

Mechanics — Asking for both a ratio (50:50) and an absolute figure (200 TMC) is a deliberate double demand — a ratio alone means nothing if the total pool of water is small, so Telangana is trying to lock in a minimum volume regardless of how the ratio fight resolves.

Incentives — A 50:50 ask is itself a negotiating position, not a legally settled entitlement (see S12) — Telangana has pushed this framing consistently since well before this year, because no tribunal has ever fixed a Telangana-specific share (see Section 4).

Numbers — 200 TMC and a 50:50 ratio; both figures per Deccan Chronicle/V6 Velugu reporting on the meeting, not independently verified against KRMB’s own minutes (which are not public).
S3 · Andhra Pradesh’s counter-ask: 66% for AP, 34% for Telangana
Andhra Pradesh’s Engineer-in-Chief K. Narasimha Murthy demanded 66% of available water for AP and only 34% for Telangana, rejecting the 50:50 framing outright.

Plain English — AP wants roughly two-thirds of the water, leaving Telangana with about a third.

Mechanics — AP’s number tracks closer to the pre-bifurcation historical usage pattern of undivided Andhra Pradesh’s Krishna allocation (see S12/Section 4) — AP’s argument, in essence, is that past usage and larger irrigated command area justify a bigger share, not a clean population-based or equal split.

Incentives — With no binding Telangana-specific tribunal award to point to, both states are arguing from whichever precedent favours them — AP from historical drawal patterns, Telangana from equity/parity — and the KRMB committee has no independent legal power to pick a winner between the two framings.

Numbers — 66:34; per Deccan Chronicle and Siasat, both reporting the same figures from Friday’s meeting.
S4 · AP’s misuse accusation: hydropower over drinking/irrigation purpose
Andhra Pradesh alleged that Telangana is drawing water released from Nagarjuna Sagar for power generation, rather than for the drinking-water or irrigation purposes the release was meant for.

Plain English — AP says Telangana is running water through its hydropower turbines and counting that as a legitimate draw, when the water was supposed to be earmarked for other uses.

Mechanics — Nagarjuna Sagar has a hydroelectric power station built into the dam; running water through its turbines to generate electricity still counts as water “used,” even though the water itself continues downstream afterward — which is exactly why the two states dispute whether hydropower releases should count against a state’s water-sharing quota at all.

Incentives — Telangana has a real incentive to maximise hydropower generation from any water it can access, since it reduces the state’s own power-purchase costs — a benefit AP does not share, which is exactly why AP frames this as misuse rather than a legitimate co-benefit of a release AP itself needed for another purpose.

Numbers — Not quantified in available Friday-meeting reporting; this is a qualitative allegation, not a metered figure, in the sources reviewed.
S5 · Telangana’s misuse accusation: over-drawal via Pothireddypadu
Telangana alleged that Andhra Pradesh is drawing more water than it should through the Pothireddypadu Head Regulator (PRP), a structure on the Srisailam reservoir’s AP side.

Plain English — Telangana says AP is quietly taking far more water out through one particular canal gate than it is entitled to.

Mechanics — PRP was originally approved at a small capacity (reported as 1,500 cusecs, meant to help supply Chennai’s drinking water) but was built and later expanded well beyond that — Telangana Today reports an initial construction capacity of 11,150 cusecs, an expansion to over 55,000 cusecs by 2006, and Telangana alleges a further illegal push toward 80,000 cusecs to move Krishna water outside the basin entirely, into the Pennar river system. A bigger regulator gate physically allows a much larger, faster draw of water whenever AP chooses to open it.

Incentives — A larger PRP capacity lets AP draw water quickly during short windows when Srisailam has surplus inflow, effectively banking water for its own delta and Rayalaseema needs before Telangana’s downstream claims are settled — which is precisely the “get there first” dynamic Telangana says the KRMB has never adequately policed.

Numbers — Telangana Today separately reports that in the 2025–26 water year, AP had drawn roughly 533–535 TMC from joint Krishna projects (about 195 TMC of it via PRP alone) against Telangana’s 117–120 TMC — a Telangana-sourced figure, not independently confirmed by AP or by KRMB’s own public data, and flagged here as one-sided.
S6 · The Board’s actual ruling: drinking water first, not irrigation
Unable to resolve the ratio dispute, the Board directed that whatever water is currently available be used first to meet drinking-water needs across both states, ahead of farm irrigation, citing El Niño-linked drought risk as the justification.

Plain English — Instead of picking a winner in the 50:50-versus-66:34 fight, the Board sidestepped it by saying: whatever little water there is, give it to people’s taps first, and worry about farm canals later.

Mechanics — This is a standard, legally uncontroversial drought-management default — drinking water is treated as the first priority use of any river water in Indian water law and practice, ahead of irrigation and industry, precisely so that a ratio dispute between states does not leave anyone without water to drink.

Incentives — A drinking-water-first order is the path of least resistance for the KRMB committee itself — it lets the Board avoid taking sides on the harder political ratio question while still being seen to act responsibly given a possible drought year.

Numbers — Not quantified with a specific TMC figure for drinking-water allocation in Friday’s reporting; a broadly similar drinking-water-priority directive was also reported around the 24 August meeting, suggesting this is the Board’s repeated fallback position, not a one-off.
S7 · The fallback instruction: just follow the 2014 Act until next time
With no ratio agreement reached, the Board told both states to operate strictly within the terms of the 2014 Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act until the next meeting, expected in the last week of September 2026.

Plain English — The Board effectively said “go back to the existing law and behave, we’ll try again in about three weeks.”

Mechanics — Because the 2014 Act itself does not spell out an exact Krishna-water ratio between AP and Telangana (see S12), telling both states to “follow the Act” is less a firm resolution than an instruction to avoid unilateral action while the underlying ratio question remains genuinely unsettled.

Incentives — Deferring to a follow-up meeting lets both Engineers-in-Chief report back to their political leadership without having conceded ground, buying roughly three weeks before the next round of pressure.

Numbers — Next meeting: “last week of September” 2026 — no exact date confirmed in available reporting.

Chandrashekar Rao’s (KCR’s) famous 2003 march on the identical route over the identical issue.

Plain English — One day before the KRMB meeting, the opposition leader publicly set a ten-day countdown for the government to release irrigation water, and threatened a protest march that deliberately recreates his father’s most famous farmer-politics moment if it doesn’t.

Mechanics — A padayatra (see glossary) is a slow, visible, multi-day walking protest designed to maximise local media coverage and farmer participation along the route — re-walking KCR’s exact 2003 route is a direct, unmistakable symbolic callback rather than a coincidence.

Incentives — KTR gains politically either way: if the government announces a release schedule by 14 September, BRS can claim credit for forcing the government’s hand; if it doesn’t, BRS gets a high-visibility farmer march that recreates its founding political myth, at a moment when it is out of power and needs a mobilising cause.

Numbers — KTR is reported (per Telangana Today and related coverage) to have cited roughly 180 TMC of water available in the Nagarjuna Sagar reservoir at the time of his speech — this is KTR’s own public claim, not an independently confirmed reservoir-level figure, and is flagged here as a political statement rather than a verified number.
S9 · The direct collision: drinking-water-first vs. a 14 September irrigation deadline
The KRMB’s Friday directive — that available water go to drinking-water needs before irrigation — sits in direct tension with KTR’s demand that the Telangana government announce a Left Canal irrigation-release schedule within ten days.

Plain English — The water board just told the government to prioritise taps over farms, on the exact same week an opposition leader is demanding the government commit to releasing water to farms on a fixed date.

Mechanics — The Telangana government does not unilaterally control how much Krishna water it has to allocate — that supply question runs through the still-unresolved KRMB ratio fight (S2/S3) — so even if the government wanted to announce a generous Left Canal schedule to defuse KTR’s threat, it would first need water actually allocated to it that a KRMB-level drinking-water-first order does not guarantee.

Incentives — This squeeze is politically convenient for BRS and awkward for the Congress state government: any shortfall in the Left Canal release can now be blamed either on AP’s share of the water or on the KRMB’s drinking-water order, but the government still bears the political cost of farmers not getting water on schedule.

Numbers — Ten days separate KTR’s 14 September deadline from Friday’s 4 September KRMB meeting; the next KRMB meeting itself is not expected until the last week of September, meaning the KTR deadline will almost certainly arrive before any fresh KRMB ratio decision.

Telangana’s Irrigation Minister, N. Uttam Kumar Reddy, is the political head overseeing this dispute, even though Friday’s KRMB meeting itself was conducted at the technical Engineer-in-Chief level, not by the minister personally.

Plain English — The engineers argue the numbers in the room; the minister is the one who will ultimately have to defend the outcome, and decide the Left Canal release schedule KTR is demanding, in public.

Mechanics — In India’s administrative structure, a department’s Engineer-in-Chief runs technical operations, while the Minister sets policy direction and answers politically for outcomes — so Uttam Kumar Reddy, not Ramesh Babu, is the one KTR’s deadline is actually aimed at.

Incentives — As a Congress minister representing a Nalgonda-district political base himself (he has previously represented both Kodad and Huzurnagar constituencies — see Section 2 dossier), Uttam Kumar Reddy has a direct personal political stake in how the Left Canal issue plays out in his own home region.

Numbers — Not applicable; this is a role/position fact, not a figure.
S11 · Whose farmland is actually on the line: the Left Canal command area
The Nagarjuna Sagar Left Canal runs through Nalgonda district, including Miryalaguda (where KTR made his announcement), irrigating farmland that depends entirely on scheduled dam releases rather than on any independent water source of its own.

Plain English — The farmers actually affected by all of this live along one specific canal in one specific district, and if the water doesn’t come on schedule, they have no backup source for their crops.

Mechanics — A canal command area (see glossary) is entirely dependent on the dam operator’s release decisions — unlike a farmer with an independent borewell, a Left Canal farmer’s kharif-season planting and harvest timing is dictated by when, and how much, water the government chooses to release.

Incentives — This total dependency is exactly what gives a Left Canal water shortfall its political force — it is not a diffuse, hard-to-see harm, but a concentrated, visible one in a specific district with a specific, mobilisation-ready farmer base (as 2003 already demonstrated).

Numbers — Not independently quantified (acreage/farmer count) in available reporting for this specific 2026 episode; not invented here.
S12 · The legal architecture behind all of this
The KRMB itself was created under the Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014, specifically to manage joint Krishna river infrastructure after Telangana’s creation split undivided Andhra Pradesh’s water entitlement between two states; separately, Article 262 of the Constitution allows Parliament to route interstate river disputes through special tribunals and boards rather than ordinary courts, which is the constitutional basis for the entire tribunal/board system (via the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956) that governs this fight.

Plain English — The rulebook that created this Board, and the constitutional reason ordinary courts mostly stay out of river disputes, both exist — but neither one actually specifies a Telangana-versus-AP split ratio.

Mechanics — Article 262(2) specifically lets Parliament bar the Supreme Court and other courts from even hearing certain interstate water disputes, pushing them instead into tribunals set up under the 1956 Act — which is why this entire fight is being fought in a board meeting room in Hyderabad, not in front of a judge.

Incentives — Because no court can simply rule on the ratio, and no tribunal has ever issued a Telangana-specific Krishna award (see Section 4), the KRMB committee has no higher authority to appeal to for a binding number — every meeting is, structurally, a negotiation between two states with a technical referee who cannot force a decision on either side.

Numbers — Not applicable; this is the legal framework, not a figure.

3 · Glossary — every term, simply

KRMB (Krishna River Management Board) The referee body set up by the Centre to manage how Andhra Pradesh and Telangana share the Krishna river’s water and infrastructure after Telangana split off in 2014 — think of it as a joint water-management committee neither state fully trusts, meeting in Hyderabad.
TMC (thousand million cubic feet) The standard unit Indian water engineers use to measure large volumes of river or reservoir water — roughly 28.3 billion litres per TMC; when Telangana asks for “200 TMC,” it is asking for a specific, very large volume, not a percentage.
Cusec Short for “cubic feet per second” — a measure of how fast water is flowing or being released at any given moment, as opposed to TMC, which measures total volume over time; a bigger regulator gate (like Pothireddypadu’s expanded capacity) can push more cusecs through at once.
Left Canal / Right Canal (Nagarjuna Sagar) The two main irrigation canals fed by the Nagarjuna Sagar dam — the Left Canal runs into Telangana’s Nalgonda and Suryapet districts, the Right Canal into Andhra Pradesh’s Guntur and Prakasam districts; each canal’s farmers depend entirely on the dam operator’s release schedule for their own side.
Canal command area The stretch of farmland that a particular canal is designed to irrigate — farmers inside a command area typically have no independent water source of their own, so a delayed or reduced canal release directly threatens their crop, unlike a farmer relying on a private borewell.
Engineer-in-Chief (EiC) The most senior technical (non-political) engineering official in a state’s irrigation department — the person who actually represents a state at technical water-sharing meetings like this one, while the Minister sets overall policy.
El Niño A periodic warming of the central Pacific Ocean that tends to weaken India’s southwest monsoon in many years, raising the risk of below-normal rainfall and drought — officials on both sides cited El Niño-linked drought risk this week, though nationally the 2026 monsoon deficit has reportedly been more severe in central and northern India than in parts of the southern peninsula including Telangana, per Down To Earth’s reporting — a nuance worth noting rather than simply accepting either state’s framing at face value.
Article 262 of the Constitution A constitutional provision letting Parliament create special tribunals to decide interstate river-water disputes, and even bar the Supreme Court and other courts from hearing such disputes at all — it is the reason this fight is being fought in a KRMB meeting room rather than a courtroom.
Inter-State River Water Disputes Act, 1956 The law Parliament passed using its Article 262 power, setting up the actual machinery (tribunals, and later boards like KRMB) for resolving river disputes between states — the Bachawat and Brijesh Kumar tribunals (Section 4) were both created under this Act.
Andhra Pradesh Reorganisation Act, 2014 The Act of Parliament that split undivided Andhra Pradesh into Andhra Pradesh and Telangana in June 2014, and which also created the KRMB (and a Godavari counterpart) to manage shared river infrastructure between the two new states going forward.
Krishna Water Disputes Tribunal (KWDT) The formal name for the two successive tribunals — Bachawat (1969) and Brijesh Kumar (2004–2010) — that have adjudicated Krishna river shares among Maharashtra, Karnataka and undivided Andhra Pradesh; neither tribunal has ever adjudicated a Telangana-specific share, since Telangana did not exist as a separate state at either time.
Tribunal award The final, legally binding decision a water disputes tribunal issues after hearing all the states involved — unlike a KRMB committee meeting, a tribunal award is meant to settle the underlying legal entitlement, not just manage year-to-year operations.
Water year The 12-month period (typically June to May, aligned with the monsoon cycle) Indian irrigation departments use to measure a season’s total water usage — figures like “533 TMC drawn in the 2025–26 water year” refer to this cycle, not the calendar year.
Gazette notification An official, legally effective public announcement published by the Government of India in its Gazette — KRMB’s formal jurisdiction over 35 named Krishna-basin projects only became legally operative once notified this way, in July 2021.
Drinking water priority (in drought) The standard rule that, when water is scarce, releasing it for people’s drinking and domestic use takes precedence over irrigation or industrial use — the principle the KRMB invoked this week to sidestep the harder ratio dispute.
Padayatra A long-distance walking march, usually over several days, undertaken as a form of political protest or campaigning in India — KCR’s 2003 Kodad-to-Haliya padayatra over this exact Left Canal water issue helped build his political profile ahead of the Telangana statehood movement; KTR is now threatening to repeat it.
Hydropower / hydroelectric generation Generating electricity by running water through turbines inside a dam — the water itself is not consumed and continues downstream, but the release is still counted as a “use” of that water for water-sharing accounting purposes, which is why AP is disputing Telangana’s hydropower draws at Nagarjuna Sagar.

🧠 Learn the concepts (from zero)

Krishna River Management Board (KRMB)
When Andhra Pradesh was split into Telangana and residual Andhra Pradesh in 2014, the Krishna river’s dams and canals suddenly belonged to two different states instead of one. The KRMB is the referee body Parliament set up under the 2014 Reorganisation Act to decide, project by project, how much water each state can draw, since the two states themselves keep disagreeing.
TMC (thousand million cubic feet)
This is the standard unit Indian irrigation departments use to measure huge volumes of reservoir water — one TMC is roughly 28,300 million litres, enough to supply a mid-sized city for weeks. When you read that Telangana wants “200 TMC,” think of it as asking for a very large, specific slice of the shared reservoir, not a vague amount.
Left Canal vs Right Canal (Nagarjuna Sagar)
The Nagarjuna Sagar dam feeds two separate canal systems. The Left Canal runs through Nalgonda district (including Miryalaguda) and parts of what is now Andhra Pradesh, irrigating farmland that depends entirely on scheduled water releases from the dam — which is exactly the water BRS is fighting over.
Interstate river water disputes
India’s Constitution (Article 262) lets Parliament keep interstate river disputes out of ordinary courts and instead route them through special mechanisms — tribunals or boards like the KRMB. This is why a purely factual, technical body (not a court, not the Centre alone) is the one issuing directions here.
Why drinking water gets priority in a drought year
El Niño is a global weather pattern linked to weaker monsoons in parts of India. When officials expect less rain, water managers reserve reservoir stock for drinking water first (a basic need with no substitute) before farm irrigation (where farmers can sometimes use groundwater or delay sowing), which is why the KRMB pushed irrigation release down the priority list this time.

🧠 Learn the concepts (from zero)

Cabinet reshuffle vs Cabinet expansion
An expansion adds new ministers to fill empty seats without removing anyone else; a reshuffle also changes who holds which department (portfolio). Telangana is expected to do a bit of both — fill three empty berths and possibly reassign some existing ministers’ departments.
The Governor’s role in appointing and removing ministers
Under Article 164 of the Constitution, ministers are appointed and can be removed by the Governor, but only on the Chief Minister’s advice — the Governor doesn’t decide on their own. That is why Revanth had to formally write to Governor Shiv Pratap Shukla to remove Konda Surekha; the Governor’s signature makes it official, but the political decision was Revanth’s (with high-command approval).
State Planning Board
This is an advisory body, not part of the Council of Ministers, that helps the state government think through long-term economic planning. Its Vice-Chairman post is a political appointment (often used to accommodate a senior leader) but carries no direct administrative power over departments like Revenue or Housing.
TG-iPASS
Telangana State Industrial Project Approval and Self-Certification System — a single-window portal that lets industrial investors get most state clearances quickly, sometimes through self-certification, instead of applying to multiple departments separately. The government cites total investment cleared through it as a headline achievement number.
Per capita income as a political number
Per capita income is a state’s total income divided by its population — a rough average, not a typical person’s actual earning. Governments like to cite it because a rising number sounds like broad prosperity, even though it says nothing about how income is distributed.
Leader of Opposition
The Leader of Opposition (LoP) is the head of the largest party outside the ruling side in the Assembly, formally recognised for privileges like an official salary and a role in some appointment committees. KCR holds this post as BRS chief, which is why Congress is pressuring him specifically to show up in the House.

Titagarh Rail Systems (TRSL) — the Indian rail-coach manufacturer, working with China’s CRRC, supplying the new DTG trains to BMRCL.

🧠 Learn the concepts (from zero)

CMRS (Commissioner of Metro Railway Safety)
Think of this as the “occupancy certificate” authority, but for trains and metro lines instead of buildings. Just as a builder cannot let residents move into a new apartment block without a municipal occupancy certificate confirming the building is safe, a metro operator cannot run a new train (or open a new stretch of track) until CMRS — a safety regulator kept independent from the railway/metro operator itself — has inspected it and signed off. CMRS sits under the Ministry of Civil Aviation, not the Railway Ministry, precisely so the inspector isn’t marking its own homework.
DTG (Distance-To-Go) signalling
A newer type of automatic train-control technology that constantly calculates the exact safe distance a train can travel before it must slow or stop, rather than relying only on fixed track-side signal blocks. It allows trains to run closer together safely, which is one way metro systems increase how many trains per hour a line can handle without laying new track.
Crush load
The maximum number of passengers a train coach or rake can carry when people are standing packed together, well beyond comfortable seated-plus-standing capacity. When Bengaluru Metro trains hit their crush load of 2,004 passengers at peak hours, it means the system is being used well past its comfortable design capacity — the technical way of describing what commuters experience as being unable to board, or being packed in like sardines.

🎯 IAS Pointer

GS Paper III (Infrastructure: Energy, Ports, Roads, Airports, Railways) and GS Paper II (government policies for urban transport). Relevant framework: the statutory role of the Commissioner of Railway Safety under the Railways Act, 1989 in certifying new lines/rolling stock. A recurring UPSC-relevant theme is urban transit governance — the separation of the operator (BMRCL, a state PSU) from the independent safety regulator (CMRS, under the Union government) as a check-and-balance model worth knowing for questions on urban infrastructure regulation.

⭐ Make-me-a-pro

Remember it this way: a metro line’s real capacity isn’t the day it opens, it’s the day it has enough certified trains to run often enough that people can actually get on. Bengaluru opened these lines years ago but is only now, in September 2026, getting the safety clearance for trains that ease the crush — a reminder that when you value land near any transit line, Bengaluru or Hyderabad, check fleet and frequency roadmaps, not just line-opening dates, before betting on the transit premium.

UDR patta vs regular patta: what “updating of registry” means for your title

Land School — an original Deccan Ledger lesson · source & further reading: UDR Patta vs Regular Patta: What Updating of Registry Actually Means for Your Title — 1acre.in

Key Highlights

  • UDR = Updating of Revenue Records (also read as Updating of Registry) — Tamil Nadu’s modernisation drive from 1981, replacing handwritten records with computerised ones.
  • It touched the whole document cluster: patta, chitta, A-Register, FMB — a full resurvey and re-recording, not a renaming.
  • A regular (pre-UDR) patta stays legally valid — but creates real friction at banks, SROs and courts because old entries no longer line up with digital databases.
  • The digitisation created its own defects: fraudulent name insertions, wrong subdivisions, classification errors — the CAG found record-vs-register mismatches in 61% of sampled villages.
  • Over 3.22 lakh private parcels were wrongly captured as GOVERNMENT land during computerisation — many still unresolved.

What UDR patta stands for

A patta created or updated under Tamil Nadu’s Updating of Revenue Records initiative of the early 1980s. The acronym matters less than the scope: this was a FULL resurvey and re-recording of village land documents — ownership entries in the A-Register, chitta classification, FMB sketches, and the patta itself — all linked into a new computerised system between 1981 and 1989. Pattas that came out of that machine carry the UDR label. (The story is Tamil Nadu’s, but the pattern — digitisation drives creating both convenience and new errors — is every state’s, Telangana’s Dharani included.)

Definition — A-Register / chitta / FMB: the village-level record cluster: the A-Register holds ownership and classification entries; the chitta the landholder-wise account; the FMB the measurement sketch per survey number. UDR rewired all three into one database.

Regular patta vs UDR patta in practice

A regular patta is not a separate legal category — simply a patta issued BEFORE UDR, recorded by hand in the Village Administrative Officer’s register and never migrated. Both confirm revenue ownership. The difference is friction: one lives in the current digital system with matching numbers; the other references a world the system no longer displays.

What the exercise actually changed

Between 1985 and 1987, new survey and subdivision numbers went out; FMB sketches were redrawn to current measurements; A-Register ownership entries were transcribed from old Karanam records into the database. The practical consequence: a 1960 patta citing Survey No. 45/2 may now correspond to 45/2A — or a split into 45/2A and 45/2B. The pre-UDR paper you hold can reference a number that NO LONGER EXISTS in the system; try to register a sale or raise a loan and that mismatch becomes a reconciliation process that eats weeks.

The errors that came with the update

Two distinct families. Clerical: transcribing decades of handwriting at speed across thousands of villages produced mismatched names, transposed survey numbers, wrong classifications — “nanjai” (wet) land showing digitally as “punjai” (dry), names spelled differently in deed and patta. The CAG’s audit found digitised-record-vs-physical-A-Register mismatches in 61% of sampled villages, and over 3.22 lakh private parcels wrongly captured as government land. Fraudulent: tenants, encroachers and third parties got names slipped into UDR pattas for land they never owned — sometimes with revenue-official help. Courts have handled this repeatedly: genuine owners discovering a stranger’s name years later, forced to the District Revenue Officer to cancel the entry. A 2016 public-interest litigation before the Madras High Court’s Madurai bench pointed at the 1985–87 resurvey as the source of large-scale discrepancies, compounded by the 2003 computerisation layering on fresh transcription errors.

What it means for transactions today

A UDR patta — reflected in the digital database with matching survey number, owner name and extent — is now the BASELINE expectation at every stage: banks verify it before home, crop or property loans; sub-registrars check it at registration, and a deed-vs-portal mismatch draws delay or objection. A regular patta triggers the longer chain — the legal team tracing the pre-UDR record forward into the current system before anything moves.

Example: Two adjacent sellers: one’s UDR patta matches the eServices portal — loan sanctioned in three weeks; the other holds a 1972 manual patta on a renumbered survey — six weeks of Tahsildar-office reconciliation before the bank even ORDERS valuation. Same land quality; the record’s format set the timeline.

How to verify and correct

Start at the state portal (Tamil Nadu: eservices.tn.gov.in) — district, taluk, village, survey number → the current patta-chitta extract. Check owner name, subdivision, extent and classification against your deed and any manual patta. Run the same check on the official land-records portal — errors visible there can trip automated verification later even when your paper is clean. Wrong name in your UDR patta? File a written representation with the revenue authority, backed by deed, EC and original patta; it escalates to the Revenue Divisional Officer, with the District Revenue Officer holding final authority on UDR corrections — and where FRAUD is involved, a court declaration may be needed to null the entry first.

Quick answers (FAQ)

Is a regular patta still valid? Yes — valid but friction-prone. Why do banks prefer UDR? Machine-checkable against the live database. Who corrects mistakes? Revenue authority → RDO → DRO; courts where fraud is involved. Someone else’s name in my patta? Written representation with your documents, immediately — these entries harden with time. Check online? eservices.tn.gov.in (TN) and the state land-records portal.

Bottom line

Both pattas confirm the same revenue ownership through different systems — but neither guarantees airtight rights: the digitisation fixed the format problem and minted its own errors, clerical and fraudulent, still surfacing decades later. Check your record against the portal BEFORE you register, sell or borrow — not when the counter clerk does it for you.

For you: The transferable rule: every digitisation wave — TN’s UDR then, Telangana’s Dharani now — creates a generation of record errors, and the owners who WIN are the ones who audit their own entries early. Audit every survey number you hold against Dharani today; and when buying, treat a seller whose paper predates the current system as a longer timeline, priced accordingly. Power of attorney for property: why a GPA sale is risky

Land School — an original Deccan Ledger lesson · source & further reading: Power of attorney for property: why a GPA sale is risky — 1acre.in

Key Highlights

  • A PoA creates an AGENCY: someone may act on the owner’s behalf. It transfers no ownership, ever.
  • Suraj Lamp v. State of Haryana (Supreme Court, 2011): only a registered sale deed conveys immovable property.
  • A 2025 Supreme Court ruling repeated it: a GPA — even paired with an agreement to sell — does not create title.
  • Genuine GPAs remain perfectly valid for MANAGEMENT — the classic case being NRIs and family members handling property from afar.
  • A deal where the GPA is the transaction itself is a deal to pause and lawyer-check.

What a power of attorney for property is

One person — the principal — signs a document authorising another — the agent (attorney holder) — to act for them on a specific property: collect rent, pay dues, sign routine paperwork, represent them before authorities when they cannot be present. The land stays the principal’s throughout.

Definition — Principal / agent: the owner giving the authority, and the person receiving it. Every power the agent has flows from the principal — and stops when the principal cancels it or dies.

What a legitimate GPA typically covers

A clean GPA spells out a limited, specific set of powers, never a vague catch-all. Read it for five things: scope — does it name the property, survey number, and the exact permitted actions? Sale authorisation — explicit words allowing price negotiation, deed execution and receiving payment (general “manage my affairs” language is NOT enough to sell); duration — open-ended, or tied to one period or transaction? Registration details — which SRO registered the GPA itself, and its number; revocation clause — how the principal can cancel it.

When a GPA is genuinely needed

Owners cannot attend every signature and filing. Legitimate uses cluster at three stages: pre-purchase — an NRI authorising a relative to negotiate while abroad; registration — an owner who cannot travel authorising someone to complete SRO formalities; post-purchase — ongoing management, rent, taxes, maintenance. The problem begins the moment a GPA is used INSTEAD of a sale deed — as a way to skip stamp duty and keep the deal off the record.

Example: The old “GPA sale”: seller hands over a GPA + agreement to sell, buyer pays full price, both save duty, nothing registers as a transfer. Cheap and invisible — until an heir, a creditor or the seller himself reappears. Everything that made it cheap made it worthless.

A GPA is right when someone must act for an owner. A registered deed is the ONLY right instrument when ownership itself must move. Treating the two as interchangeable is where most GPA disputes are born.

Is a PoA reliable?

Only as reliable as its registration and the principal’s continuing authority. The classic failures: relying on an old GPA nobody confirmed was unrevoked; accepting a notarised-only document where registration was required; assuming a GPA survives the principal’s death (it does not — agency ends at death, with a narrow exception where the agent holds a genuine interest in the property). After Suraj Lamp, several states issued circulars tightening registration practice — another reason to verify at the SRO rather than trust the paper shown.

Definition — Agency coupled with interest: the narrow exception where the agent has a real stake in the property itself — such authority can survive revocation and death. It is the legitimate backbone of a builder’s PoA inside a registered JDA.

Pre-purchase checklist

☐ GPA registered at the SRO — note the number.
☐ It explicitly authorises sale, price negotiation and deed execution.
☐ The principal personally confirms it is unrevoked (a video call is cheap).
☐ The principal was alive and competent when it was signed — and is alive now.
☐ The actual transfer happens by registered sale deed, with the principal as vendor.
☐ A property lawyer reads any deal where a GPA carries real weight.

Quick answers (FAQ)

Is a GPA sale legal in India? As a transfer of ownership — no. Since Suraj Lamp (2011), only a registered deed conveys property.

Can a GPA holder sell on the owner’s behalf? Yes — IF the GPA expressly authorises sale, and the deed is registered naming the principal as seller. The holder signs FOR the owner; he never becomes the owner.

What happens to a GPA when the principal dies? It dies too. Anything signed after is void — except the narrow coupled-with-interest case.

Does a PoA transfer ownership? Never, however it is worded.

How do I verify a GPA before a purchase? Registration at the SRO, express powers in the text, principal alive and confirming — the three checks that cover almost everything.

Bottom line

A power of attorney is a genuinely useful management tool that was never designed to replace a sale deed. Buyers who keep that line bright — and verify registration and current validity before relying on one — avoid nearly all the GPA grief that ends up in court.

For you: In your JD work, PoAs are daily equipment — landowners grant you development authority, NRI owners send registration authority. Draft them task-scoped and registered; and when one appears on the OTHER side of the table, run the three checks before the price conversation.
