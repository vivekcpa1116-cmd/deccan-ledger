Concept Library Vivek’s growing textbook

Land Titles & Registration

Verbal instructions vs. written guidelines (G.O.) NEW
A Government Order is published, numbered and citable; a verbal instruction exists only in the memory of whichever official handles your file. When a department is fixing a land-record error through oral instructions rather than a written order — as revenue officials reportedly are on Section 22A — two identical properties can get two different outcomes depending purely on who answers the phone. — taught by: the 22A crisis’s verbal-corrections report
The three-bottleneck problem in land acquisition NEW
Land acquisition (paying for and transferring title), R&R (resettling displaced people) and forest clearance (where an alignment crosses forest land) are three separate approval chains that must all clear before a project proceeds — a government “war footing” order is only as fast as its weakest chain, usually R&R or forest clearance, not the acquisition paperwork itself. — taught by: the Irrigation Minister’s land-acquisition war-footing order
Registration — why it’s everything
You legally own immovable property only once the sale deed is registered with stamp duty paid — not merely once you’ve paid for it. Block registration and you’ve effectively frozen the property. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Prohibited list (Section 22A)
A district-wise register of survey numbers sub-registrars must refuse to register — legitimately covering government, endowment/wakf and ceiling-surplus land, but dangerous because it works on survey numbers, which can wrongly sweep up private land sharing or mistyped into a listed number. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Colony-level over-listing (22A)
The specific harm Harish Rao attacked: instead of one stray survey number, entire registered residential colonies get added to the 22A list in a batch — freezing hundreds of clean, patta homes at once so owners can’t sell or raise a loan. — taught by: Harish Rao’s 22A Assembly threat
Relaxation / release rate
The government’s own scorecard for a prohibited-list crisis: of the total relaxation (release) applications filed, how many are actually cleared versus rejected. Telangana’s Revenue Minister quoting “98% cleared, only 12–13 of 300+ rejected” is this exact metric — a number worth checking again once independent audits exist, since a self-reported clearance rate is not the same as a verified one. — taught by: Ponguleti’s 22A rebuttal
De-notification
The formal process of striking a property off a prohibited/restricted list so it becomes transactable again — the exact step the alleged “30% commission” racket sits on top of. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Patta land
Land for which the owner holds a formal government-issued title deed, as opposed to disputed, assigned, or government land — the category Section 22A is meant to exclude, and the category getting wrongly trapped by it. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Regularisation, GOs and memos
Governments legalise occupied or exempted land over the decades through Government Orders — but a regularised parcel is only clean on paper if that GO is attached to its record. Collect the GO/memo history, not just the sale deed. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
TSLR vs GHMC numbering
Hyderabad has two parallel address systems — a 1969–70 Town Survey Land Records numbering and GHMC’s own ward/block numbers. Mismatches between the two have turned private homes into “government land” on paper. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
ULC (Urban Land Ceiling)
A repealed law that once capped urban landholding; the exemption/surrender records it left behind still matter, since several exempted parcels were wrongly re-listed for want of an entered exemption. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Waqf land
Property permanently donated for Muslim religious purposes under a central Act — state revenue officers cannot simply delete waqf entries, so colonies near old disputed waqf land face the slowest 22A-style resolution. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Dharani vs Bhu Bharati
Dharani was the previous government’s land-records portal; Bhu Bharati is the current replacement. Errors made migrating data between the two are the government’s own explanation for the 22A crisis. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Khata (and a khata freeze)
The municipal record that puts a property on the local tax/ownership rolls — without it you can’t cleanly get a bank loan, utility connections, or resell. Freezing khata issuance on a batch of sites (as happened with 1,100 MUDA plots) makes them unsellable overnight even though the sale deed still exists. — taught by: the MUDA cancellation’s implementation
Cancellation vs title
An allotment-based title can be undone at the root: if the original government allotment was illegal, everything built on it since — deeds, registrations, loans — can collapse. Registration records a transaction; it doesn’t purify a rotten source. — taught by: the MUDA scam
Development authority (built-in conflict)
Bodies like MUDA, BDA and HMDA both make the rules (approvals) and play the game (own and sell land) — a structural conflict of interest that land scandals tend to grow from. — taught by: the MUDA scam
Alternative-site / 50:50 compensation
Paying a landowner in developed plots instead of cash when their raw land is acquired — the honest version is transparent and formula-based (like land pooling); the abused version becomes a discretionary currency for favours. — taught by: the MUDA scam
Land Pooling (60:40 model)
Telangana’s proposed statewide alternative to forced acquisition: instead of paying cash for raw land, the government “pools” it, develops the layout (roads, drainage, plots), and gives the original owner back 60% of the fully-developed plots — keeping 40% to sell and fund the infrastructure. Cleaner than 50:50 discretionary compensation because the ratio is fixed in advance, not negotiated case by case — but only as clean as the bill’s actual text turns out to be. — taught by: the Assembly’s 7 September land-pooling agenda
Commission of Inquiry
A judge-led fact-finding body that can recommend but not punish — its power is legitimacy: acting on a judge’s report is politically defensible, ignoring one is costly. — taught by: the MUDA scam
JDA and benami (the coercive version)
A Joint Development Agreement — landowner contributes land, developer builds, they split the result — is your core, legal business. The dirty cousin: get land blacklisted, owner turns desperate, a “partner” appears offering 50:50 through a benami (name-lender) entity. — taught by: the Section 22A saga
Restoration vs redevelopment
Restoration repairs what exists; redevelopment replaces what failed. Good heritage projects mix both instead of bulldozing everything. — taught by: the Mir Alam Mandi revamp
Phased execution
Build a section, shift occupants in, build the next — slower and costlier per square foot, but livelihoods survive the construction, unlike a clear-everyone-out-and-build-fast approach. — taught by: the Mir Alam Mandi revamp
Reserve price vs sale price
A published reserve is only the floor an auctioneer will accept — the actual winning bid in a competitive e-auction can land well above it, as TGIIC’s Raidurg auctions have now shown twice. — taught by: ITI’s Bengaluru land e-auction; confirmed again by the Raidurg record
PSU land monetisation / NLMC
The National Land Monetization Corporation is the central vehicle that professionally values and auctions off surplus land held by old public-sector companies inside cities that grew up around their factories/offices — a leading government-supply signal for local land markets. — taught by: ITI’s Bengaluru land e-auction
RMP zoning
Bengaluru’s statutory Revised Master Plan tags every parcel with a permitted use; a “Commercial (Business)” tag means a buyer can build offices without a fresh land-use conversion — a major value driver baked into the price. — taught by: ITI’s Bengaluru land e-auction
TGIIC vs HMDA
Two different Telangana government bodies both sell/auction Hyderabad-area land — TGIIC out of an industrial-development mandate, HMDA out of an urban-planning one. Knowing which one you’re dealing with matters, since their land banks, rules and pricing patterns differ. — taught by: the Raidurg auction record
MSTC e-auction platform
A central PSU-run online bidding portal used by many government bodies to run transparent land/asset auctions — competitively bid, published-reserve pricing that’s far harder to under-report than a privately negotiated sale, which is why auction results become the market’s reference price. — taught by: the Raidurg auction record
RERA Section 10 (agent liability)
RERA doesn’t just regulate builders — a registered real-estate agent who markets or books units in a project that isn’t itself RERA-registered can personally be ordered to refund buyers with interest. “I was just the broker” is not a defence. — taught by: a TG-RERA refund order
Alienation of protected land
In Indian land law, “alienation” means the legal transfer of ownership or rights out of a protected/public category — via sale, lease, gift, exchange or mortgage — into a different use. It doesn’t mean abandonment; it means converting the land’s legal status, which is exactly what Karnataka’s new park-land bill would let government do to up to 5% of any park, once it actually clears the Assembly. — taught by: Karnataka’s park-land alienation bill
FTL & buffer zone
Full Tank Level is the highest water level a lake, tank or river stretch is engineered to hold; the buffer zone is a protective ring of undeveloped land around it that keeps the water body’s catchment and flood path clear. Land inside either category is legally fragile — even a clean-looking patta can’t override an FTL/buffer classification once a parcel is formally surveyed into it. — taught by: the Musi land-pooling directive
TDR (Transferable Development Rights)
Instead of paying cash when the government takes your land for a public project, it can issue a TDR certificate — the right to build extra floor area on a different plot you own, or to sell that right to someone else. It only has real value if there’s a plot to use it on and a working market to sell it in — exactly why builder pushback on the Musi project has centred on TDR’s practical usability, not its legality. — taught by: the Musi land-pooling directive

Building & Apartment Law

RERA Section 8 (project takeover by allottees) NEW
When a registered project’s promoter defaults, the regulator can hand day-to-day completion to the project’s own buyers’ association instead of leaving them with only a compensation claim against a promoter who may have no money left — and a regulator can later carve out narrower relief (like registration for specific units) on a separate, faster timeline from the main completion order. — taught by: the TG-RERA Jaya Diamonds order
Encroachment vs. ownership dispute NEW
An enforcement agency’s demolition power is legally premised on land being encroached — on a lake buffer, road alignment or similarly protected category — not on ordinary private land. Whether a specific plot actually falls in such a category, or whether the boundary was misjudged, is exactly what a court case turns on, and exactly what a single complaint video cannot settle either way. — taught by: the Gandi Maisamma HYDRAA complaint
RWA (Residents’ Welfare Association) mandate
The single legally-recognised body representing all owners in a completed apartment project. Karnataka’s new law requires exactly one per project, even multi-phase ones, closing a loophole where developers split large complexes into multiple weak associations to dodge accountability. — taught by: Karnataka’s Apartment Ownership Bill
75% redevelopment consent
The ownership-majority threshold a Karnataka apartment complex now needs to approve redevelopment. Owners who refuse can still be bought out, but only at twice independently-assessed market value — protecting holdouts without letting one owner block an entire rebuild. — taught by: Karnataka’s Apartment Ownership Bill
Structural safety audit
A certified engineer’s mandatory, repeating (every 5 years) inspection for any building over 30 years old — a paper trail a buyer should now always ask for on an older Bengaluru apartment. — taught by: Karnataka’s Apartment Ownership Bill
Private-road municipal takeover
A municipality can now declare a poorly-maintained layout’s internal road a “public road” and take over its upkeep — with zero compensation to whoever owned it — the moment it’s judged a public-safety risk. — taught by: Karnataka’s municipal amendment bills
Plot-size vs sanctioned-height rule
Hyderabad’s enforcement trigger for small plots: below 75 sq. yards, anything built over 7 metres (roughly ground-plus-one) needs full building permission regardless of what was originally sanctioned; above 75 sq. yards, any construction with zero permission at all is the violation. The collapsed Anjaiah Nagar building was sanctioned for two floors and had seven built on a plot the owner only partly owned — both limits broken at once. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
Encroachment on a nala (storm-water drain)
Building even partly over a natural drainage channel is a double violation — it steals land that was never the owner’s to build on, and it removes water’s only path during heavy rain, which is why nala-encroaching structures are treated as both illegal and structurally suspect. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
HYDRAA
Hyderabad Disaster Response and Assets Monitoring and Protection Agency — a Telangana government body created to identify and demolish unsafe, illegal or encroaching structures across the city, working alongside (not instead of) the local municipal corporation’s own Town Planning wing. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
UC (unauthorised construction) notice
A formal notice a municipal Town Planning wing serves on a building it finds was constructed without permission, or beyond what was permitted — the first legal step before seizure or demolition, and the paper trail that turns a builder’s violation into an enforceable case. — taught by: the Gachibowli building collapse crackdown
Citizen-tip enforcement (MyCURE / Prajavani)
Telangana’s municipal complaint apps let any resident report suspected illegal construction with photos and a location pin; officials now actively cross-check these tips against field inspections to decide where enforcement teams go next — meaning a neighbour’s complaint can trigger a demolition notice on a small plot today, without an inspector having to spot the violation first. — taught by: Cyberabad’s widening demolition drive

Government Money & Institutions

Royalty share (mining auctions) NEW
The percentage of a mineral’s value a winning bidder pays the government as royalty — the specific commercial term that determines how profitable a block actually needs to be to justify the exploration spend behind it. SCCL committed a 37.7% royalty share for its new Karnataka gold-and-copper block. — taught by: SCCL’s Devadurga gold-copper block
Centrally-sponsored scheme funding NEW
Many large Indian welfare schemes (housing, rural employment, health) are jointly funded, with states implementing and the Centre releasing its share on its own schedule — that schedule becoming a live political flashpoint is common whenever the state and central governments are run by different parties, as with a year of zero central disbursement on Telangana’s Indiramma Housing scheme. — taught by: the Indiramma Housing funds row
Welfare-corporation scam
State governments run development corporations (for SCs, STs, minorities, backward classes) that hold budgeted welfare money in bank accounts. The classic fraud: funds are quietly transferred out of the corporation’s account into a web of shell/personal accounts, then allegedly recycled — here, ₹89 crore transferred out, an overall ~₹187 crore diversion alleged from the Valmiki (ST) Corporation. The tell is always a welfare pot with weak oversight and a minister-in-charge who controls it. — taught by: the Valmiki Corporation scam
Revenue deficit
Daily income minus daily bills, with no asset creation either side — a state running one is borrowing just to pay salaries and pensions, like taking a loan for groceries. — taught by: Telangana’s CAG report
Fiscal deficit
The full year’s borrowing need, including money spent building assets. Borrowing to build a road can pay for itself; borrowing for groceries can’t — the ratio between the two deficits tells you which kind of borrower a state is. — taught by: Telangana’s CAG report
Committed expenditure
Salaries, pensions and interest — the untouchable first claim on every rupee. When it eats the budget, infrastructure that moves land prices fights for scraps. — taught by: Telangana’s CAG report
Supplementary budget
Extra spending a government asks the legislature to approve mid-year, on top of the original annual budget — usually for unplanned or newly prioritised items. Karnataka’s ₹7,042.69-crore supplementary estimate, with ₹1,000 crore specifically for Bengaluru roads, is exactly this: money added to the year’s plan, not part of the original allocation. — taught by: Karnataka’s supplementary budget
Devolution / Finance Commission share
Every five years a Finance Commission decides what slice of central taxes each state gets. A state that grows faster and controls population better can still see its share shrink, since formulas partly weight population — Karnataka says this cost it ₹65,000 crore over six years. — taught by: the Southern Zonal Council

The central law governing who can tax and regulate mineral extraction. States want more taxing power over mining within their own territory; the Centre has historically resisted for the sake of national uniformity. — taught by: the Southern Zonal Council
Political clearance (MEA)
Before any Chief Minister travels abroad, the Ministry of External Affairs must clear both the trip and its itinerary. A single G.O. can fold entire departments or classes of institutions into a new body overnight, without a legislative vote — the same sweeping-order power that created the Section 22A mess reshaped technical education via GO 97. — taught by: the GO 97 protests

One crore acres, one verbal instruction at a time: how Telangana’s land registry broke, and why the fix keeps almost working

Deep Dive of the Day · anchor reporting: “Section 22A woes deepen as incomplete prohibitory list reappears on portal” — Deccan Chronicle, 26 Aug 2026 · cross-read with The Hans India, Telangana Today and Namasthe Telangana (BRS-aligned — bias flagged in Section 2)

1 · The story, explained from zero — fact by fact

F1 · The portal came back — but two categories are still switched off
The Section 22A prohibited-property list, which had vanished entirely from public view weeks into this crisis, reappeared on the Registration & Stamps Department’s own website on Tuesday, 25 August. Agricultural and non-agricultural prohibited entries loaded again — but clicking into the Wakf or Endowments categories returns only a placeholder: “Prohibition data is under review. It will be published soon.”

Plain English — The list of “you can’t sell this” properties, which citizens couldn’t even check anymore, is back online — except for two of its most legally tangled categories, which still show nothing.

Mechanics — The list runs on the Registration & Stamps Department’s public-facing site, built on top of the state’s new Bhu Bharati land-records system. Wakf (property permanently donated for Muslim religious purposes, governed by a central Act) and Endowments (temple trust land) both sit under separate legal custodians — the Wakf Board and the Endowments Department — whose own records must be reconciled before a sub-registrar can safely confirm a parcel’s status. That extra approval layer is almost certainly why these two categories are still dark while the rest of the list reloaded.

Incentives — Restoring most of the list, but not all of it, lets the government point to visible progress on a viral, multi-week crisis without having actually finished the underlying data reconciliation. A partial fix reads better in a headline than it functions for the owner of a Wakf-adjacent parcel who still has no way to check their own status.
F2 · The same day, two unconnected districts erupted in sit-in protests
On Tuesday, landowners in Karimnagar and Vikarabad — districts with no obvious geographic or administrative link to each other — staged dharnas demanding their properties be struck off the prohibited list immediately, saying the freeze has cost them the ability to sell or mortgage land they legitimately own.

Plain English — While the portal was quietly being patched in Hyderabad, angry owners were physically sitting outside government offices in two different parts of the state on the very same day.

Mechanics — A dharna is a sit-in protest at a government office, a standard Indian escalation tool when routine grievance channels haven’t worked — it forces local officials to notice a problem and pass it up the chain rather than let it sit in a queue.

Numbers — Two different districts protesting on the same day, independently, is the tell that this isn’t a single confused village — it’s simultaneous frustration boiling over in unconnected corners of the state, which is a very different, much harder problem for a government to manage than one local flare-up.
F3 · Corrections are reportedly being made through verbal instructions, not published rules
A Telugu-language report (Namasthe Telangana, BRS-aligned — see Section 2 for the bias note) cited revenue officials describing a corrections process run on oral instructions rather than any published, written guideline for how a wrongly-listed parcel gets removed from the prohibited list.

Plain English — Officials are reportedly being told what to do by phone or in meetings, not through a written order anyone outside the room could point to or rely on.

Mechanics — A Government Order (G.O.) is published, numbered and citable — a citizen, lawyer or journalist can look it up and hold the government to its own stated rule. A verbal instruction exists only in the memory and discretion of whichever official happens to be handling your file that day. Two identical properties can get two different outcomes depending purely on who answers the phone.

Incentives — Verbal instructions are politically convenient precisely because they’re deniable and revisable, and they leave no paper trail an RTI request or a court could later use to establish exactly what the government knew and decided, and when. Whether that’s deliberate or simply institutional haste under pressure, the practical effect on an affected owner is identical: no accountability trail to point to.
F4 · The scale, if the reported numbers hold: more than a third of the state’s land
Reporting citing revenue-adjacent sources put roughly 76,000 acres locked up in Jangaon and Hanumakonda districts alone, and a separately circulated figure puts the statewide prohibited-list total above one crore acres.

Numbers — Telangana’s total geographic area is roughly 2.75 crore acres. If the one-crore-acre statewide figure is even directionally accurate, it would mean more than a third of the entire state’s land is currently flagged as “prohibited” in some form. Even a figure discounted by half would still describe a crisis on a scale that explains four weeks of Cabinet-level attention, rather than a local irritant fading on its own.

Mechanics — These figures come from cross-referenced press reporting, not an official government release — no authoritative acreage number has been published by the state itself. That absence is its own small scandal sitting inside the larger one: the government hasn’t told citizens how big the problem it is fixing actually is.
F5 · The root cause: an old, dirty list got carried into a new, faster system
Officials and independent reporting trace the crisis to Telangana’s migration of land records from the old Dharani portal to the new Bhu Bharati system, begun in April 2025 — during which outdated prohibited-list entries, some rooted in litigation and Government Orders that predate the current government, were pushed forward without being reconciled against land that had since been regularised or exempted.

Mechanics — A data migration is only as clean as the data it starts from; moving a flawed list faster and more visibly online doesn’t fix the flaws, it just makes them load quicker for everyone to hit at once.

Incentives — A new government inherited an old list and, in trying to modernise the system for political credit (Bhu Bharati was launched as a flagship land-reform initiative), imported the old errors wholesale rather than cleaning them first — a sequencing mistake that turned a legacy administrative backlog into a fresh, branded crisis under its own name.
F6 · The government’s own reassuring number doesn’t match the alarming one, and both can be true at once
Two days before this week’s events, Revenue Minister Ponguleti Srinivas Reddy said 98% of registrations sought under Section 22A relief had already been cleared. That claim sits alongside the reported one-crore-acre statewide figure with no official reconciliation between the two.

Numbers — “98% of registrations sought and cleared” is a narrower metric than “acres wrongly listed” — it only counts owners who actively applied for relief, not the full population still frozen, some of whom may not even know they’re affected yet. A high clearance RATE on a small, self-selected base is fully compatible with a huge absolute number still stuck.

Incentives — Government messaging naturally emphasises the reassuring metric; the opposition and affected owners emphasise the alarming one. Both numbers can be accurate and still describe two very different realities — which is exactly why neither, alone, tells you what you actually need to know before trusting a specific parcel’s status.
F7 · An unverified but structurally plausible allegation: paying middlemen to get de-listed
BRS has alleged that affected owners are being forced to pay intermediaries to get their properties cleared from the prohibited list — an allegation not independently verified in available reporting.

Mechanics — This is the classic rent-seeking pattern that emerges whenever a formal process is slow, opaque, and run on undocumented official discretion: the harder a system is to navigate through official channels, the more valuable an unofficial “expediter” becomes, honest or not.

Incentives — Neither the allegation nor a government denial is verified here — treat it specifically as an allegation. But the underlying conditions this deep dive has already established (an opaque, verbally-run correction process, no published rulebook) are precisely the conditions in which such a middleman market tends to grow, whether or not this specific claim is accurate.

BRS deputy floor leader T. Harish Rao has demanded a probe headed by a sitting High Court judge, a government “white paper” detailing exactly how properties were added to and removed from the list, and Revenue Minister Ponguleti Srinivas Reddy’s resignation — alongside a broader charge that the Revanth Reddy government is “land-centric,” not “people-centric.”

Mechanics — A sitting-judge probe and a published white paper are both escalation devices — they try to force the government to commit specifics on the permanent record, where they can be checked and cited later, rather than leaving the government’s account as a series of reassuring press statements.

Incentives — Whether or not BRS’s harder allegations (a ₹30,000-crore land-sale figure, land going to the CM’s relatives) hold up to scrutiny, the pressure itself is a real input into how fast the government moves — see the full political dossier in today’s Telangana Politics zone.

3 · Glossary — every term, simply

Section 22A (Registration Act) — The legal provision letting Telangana maintain a “prohibited list” of survey numbers that sub-registrars must refuse to register — legitimately meant for government land, wakf/endowment land, ceiling-surplus land and land under litigation. Example: a genuine temple-trust plot in Hyderabad correctly belongs on this list; a private house wrongly caught up in it does not.
Prohibited property / prohibited list — The specific register of survey numbers that cannot be legally registered (bought, sold or mortgaged) until removed. Being on it doesn’t mean you don’t own the land — it means you currently can’t transact it.
Bhu Bharati — Telangana’s current official land-records portal and legal framework (the Telangana Bhu Bharati Record of Rights in Land Act, 2025), replacing the earlier Dharani system.
Dharani — The previous Telangana government’s land-records portal, in use from 2020 until Bhu Bharati replaced it starting April 2025; the source system this crisis’s data errors were carried over from.
Wakf — Property permanently donated for Islamic religious or charitable purposes, governed by the central Wakf Act, 1995 and administered by a state Wakf Board — legally distinct from ordinary private property even when it looks identical on the ground.
Endowment (temple trust) land — Land or property dedicated to a Hindu temple or religious trust, administered under state Endowments law by a dedicated department — the Hindu-trust counterpart to Wakf land.
Patta / patta land — Land for which the owner holds a formal government-issued title deed — the category Section 22A is meant to exclude, and the category being wrongly caught by it in this crisis.
Ceiling-surplus land — Land a state government took over decades ago under old urban/agricultural land-ceiling laws that capped how much land one person could hold; a legitimate category for the prohibited list, but one whose old records are easy to misapply to land since regularised.
Sub-Registrar — The local government official who actually registers (records) a property sale deed; the person legally required to check the 22A list before allowing a registration to proceed — and the person on the receiving end of any verbal instruction described in F3.
Government Order (G.O.) — A formal, numbered, published instruction from a state government — citable, appealable, and part of the public record. The explicit alternative to the verbal instructions described in F3.
Dharna — A sit-in protest at a government office, used across India to force local officials to escalate an unresolved grievance rather than let it sit unaddressed.
De-listing / relaxation — The formal process of striking a wrongly-included property off the prohibited list so it becomes transactable again — the exact step this crisis has made slow, opaque and (per BRS’s unverified allegation) potentially monetised by intermediaries.
White paper (government) — A formal, published document laying out a government’s full account of a policy or crisis, including numbers and decisions — distinct from press statements, and much harder to walk back once issued.
Commission of Inquiry / sitting-judge probe — Formal fact-finding mechanisms a government can order into its own administrative failures; a sitting High Court judge carries more institutional independence than a retired-judge Commission, and is correspondingly rarer for a government to grant voluntarily.
Revenue deficit (context) — Elsewhere in this edition, Telangana’s CAG-reported revenue deficit is tracked in the Concept Library — relevant here only as background: a state managing a tight fiscal position has less administrative slack to throw extra staff at an unglamorous data-reconciliation problem like this one, which is one plausible (not confirmed) contributor to how slowly the fix is moving.

🎓 How to calculate stamp duty and registration charges on land

Land School — an original Deccan Ledger lesson · source & further reading: How to Calculate Stamp Duty and Registration Charges on Land — 1acre.in

Key Highlights

  • Stamp duty is a STATE tax on the transfer; registration charges are a separate fee for recording it.
  • Both are generally payable before the sale deed can register.
  • Rates vary by state, property type, buyer category and value — property is a state subject.
  • The base is generally the HIGHER of the sale price and the guideline/stamp-duty value.
  • Online calculators estimate the bill in advance — with current-year rates only.

The two levies, separated

Stamp duty: a statutory tax on the property transaction itself — the state’s cut for recognising the transfer. Registration charges: the fee for recording the deed in official records. Different levies, different lines on the challan; both generally must be paid before the Sub-Registrar registers your deed. Budget them together: in Telangana the combined package on a sale deed runs about 7.5% of the value.

Understanding stamp duty

Each state’s own stamp law governs — which is why rates, concessions and even calculation methods differ across borders. The two planning variables: the STATE-SPECIFIC RATE (the biggest number in your closing costs), and POTENTIAL EXEMPTIONS — many states discount duty for women buyers or first-time purchasers. Verify the current rate and valuation rules before finalising any purchase; budgets built on last year’s percentages miss.

Registration charges explained

Payable for entering the sale deed into the official record — the act that makes your ownership publicly traceable and your deed retrievable as a certified copy forever. Rates vary by state and can carry minimum or maximum limits (some states cap registration fees in absolute rupees; others charge a flat percentage — Telangana’s is 0.5%).

The calculation, step by step

The maths is simple once the base is fixed. 1) Determine the transaction value — the actual agreed price. 2) Check the guideline / stamp-duty value for the survey number. 3) The BASE is the higher of the two. 4) Apply the state’s duty rate. 5) Add registration charges. 6) Add any state surcharge or cess. The declared price never beats the guideline floor: under-declare and the department simply computes on its own benchmark.

Example: Telangana example: plot agreed at ₹80 lakh, guideline value ₹90 lakh. Base = ₹90 lakh. At ~7.5% combined, the SRO bill is ≈ ₹6.75 lakh — on the guideline figure, not the price. A buyer who budgeted 7.5% of ₹80 lakh is ₹75,000 short at the counter.

Using a calculator effectively

State portals and property sites host duty calculators: feed location and value, get the estimate instantly. Two disciplines: confirm the calculator uses THIS year’s rates (states tweak percentages in budget sessions), and use it comparatively — running the same investment across district or state lines can reveal lakhs of difference in friction cost alone.

Exemptions and reductions

State-dependent concessions exist for women purchasers, affordable-housing projects, specified FAMILY transfers (gift/settlement deeds between close relatives often carry nominal duty), and other notified categories. Always check eligibility before registration — concessions are claimed at the counter, not refunded after.

Why rates vary regionally

Property is a state subject: each government prescribes its own rates and concessions, tuned to its revenue needs, property type (commercial vs residential) and urban demand. The practical rule: never carry one state’s percentage in your head into another state’s deal — verify with the registration department that will actually stamp your deed.

Key takeaways for buyers

☐ Calculate duty BEFORE signing the agreement.
☐ Verify the guideline value for the exact survey number.
☐ Put registration charges in the budget line, not the surprise line.
☐ Check concession eligibility (women buyers, family transfers).
☐ Verify ownership documents before registration — duty paid on a bad title is money on a bad title.

Quick answers (FAQ)

Calculated on sale price or guideline value? The higher of the two. Same across states? No — rates, fees and concessions all differ. First-time buyer discounts? In some states; check the current notification. Documents needed to pay and register? The deed, identity/PAN, the challan or e-stamp, and the property’s record extracts. Why estimate in advance? Because 6–8% of a land deal is lakhs — a closing-day shortfall can cost you the slot and, with a hard deadline in the agreement, the advance.

Bottom line

Duty and registration are the certain, calculable part of a land deal — the only closing surprise they ever produce is the one a five-minute advance calculation would have removed.

For you: Quote plot prices to your buyers as “all-in at registration” maths — price + ~7.5% + mutation — in the first meeting. Transparency about the state’s cut builds the trust that closes files. On your own acquisitions, model duty into land cost per acre from day one: on a ₹10 Cr assembly it is ₹75 lakh of real money, not a rounding error. 🎓 CRZ rules: how far from the coast you can build

Land School — an original Deccan Ledger lesson · source & further reading: CRZ Rules in India: How Far From the Coast You Can Build — 1acre.in

Key Highlights

  • CRZ rules control construction near the coastline — they decide whether a coastal plot is buildable at all.
  • Four zones: CRZ-I (ecologically sensitive — essentially no building), II (urban coast), III (rural coast), IV (the water itself).
  • The 2019 notification cut the rural No Development Zone from 200 m to 50 m in dense villages — but ONLY once the state’s Coastal Zone Management Plan is approved under it; until then 200 m continues.
  • Every coastal project needs PRIOR clearance — courts have ordered completed buildings demolished for lacking it.
  • Distance is measured from the officially demarcated High Tide Line, not the visible waterline.

What the CRZ is and why it exists

The Coastal Regulation Zone is the legal strip along India’s coastline within which activity is controlled — notified under the Environment (Protection) Act, 1986, with the central environment ministry as nodal authority and state Coastal Zone Management Authorities enforcing. The balance it strikes: protecting mangroves, wetlands and dunes, protecting fishing communities’ livelihoods, and still allowing coastal development — by classifying the coast into zones, each with its own rules on construction, industry and land conversion near the high tide line.

Definition — High Tide Line (HTL): the officially DEMARCATED line the sea reaches at highest tide — mapped by authorised agencies, not eyeballed from the beach. Every setback measures from this line; measuring from the visible waterline is the classic buyer error.

The four zones

CRZ-I — ecologically sensitive: IA covers mangroves and coral reefs, IB the intertidal strip between tide lines; new construction essentially off the table, narrow exceptions for defence, atomic energy and major public infrastructure. CRZ-II — urban coast: already-developed municipal coastline; building allowed only LANDWARD of existing roads or authorised structures — never toward the sea — following local FSI/FAR. CRZ-III — rural coast, split by density (Census 2011): IIIA is dense rural coast (>2,161 people/sq km) with a 50-metre No Development Zone; IIIB is sparse coast at 200 metres. CRZ-IV — the water: IVA open sea from low tide line to 12 nautical miles, IVB tidal inland waters; traditional fishing protected, untreated discharge banned.

Definition — No Development Zone (NDZ): the strip inland from the HTL where new construction is barred regardless of ownership. Owning land inside the NDZ means owning a view, not a building site.

What changed in 2019

Three big moves. The long-standing FREEZE on Floor Space Index in eligible coastal areas was lifted — development can follow local planning regulations, subject to CRZ conditions. Clearances were DECENTRALISED — state authorities now handle much more, with the centre appraising specified categories. And the rural NDZ was cut for dense villages — but here is the catch buyers miss: the relaxations take effect only after the state’s Coastal Zone Management Plan prepared under the 2019 framework is formally approved. Until that approval, the OLD setbacks continue. A “50-metre plot” in a state with an unapproved CZMP is still a 200-metre plot.

Definition — Coastal Zone Management Plan (CZMP): the state’s official map of its coast — which stretch is which zone, where the HTL runs. The 2019 relaxations are switched on, state by state, only by its approval.

What is permanently off-limits

Whatever the zone: new industries, hazardous-substance storage, sand and coral mining, land reclamation and bunding within the regulated distance. Groundwater: no extraction within 200 m of the HTL except community use; between 200 and 500 m, only manual drawing from ordinary wells, and only where no alternative exists.

Getting clearance

Mandatory BEFORE any construction in the zone. Jurisdiction splits by category: CRZ-I and IV go to the central ministry; CRZ-II and III — covering most residential and commercial work — to the State CZMA. Small mercy for individuals: self-dwelling units up to 300 sq m get a simpler path through the local authority. Building without clearance is the one shortcut with a documented ending: courts have ordered COMPLETED buildings demolished.

Example: A buyer takes a “beach-view villa plot” 120 m from the water in a dense fishing village, told “the new rules allow 50 m”. The state’s CZMP is still pending approval — so the 200 m NDZ applies, and the plot cannot host a villa at all. The seller quoted the notification; the buyer needed the CZMP status.

Quick answers (FAQ)

Minimum distance from the sea to build a house? Zone-dependent: 50 m in approved dense rural stretches, 200 m otherwise in rural coast, road-line rule in urban CRZ-II. Is the 50 m NDZ automatic? No — only after CZMP approval. Hotels and resorts? Possible in permitted stretches with clearance, never inside the NDZ or CRZ-I. Building without clearance? Fines, prosecution — and demolition orders that survive completion.

Bottom line

On the coast, the zone IS the value: the same acre is a resort site in CRZ-II, a 200-metre wait in IIIB, and a permanent no in CRZ-I. Verify zone, CZMP status and HTL for the exact survey number before believing any price.
